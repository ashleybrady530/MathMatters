Math Structure Matters

Mathematical content can look correct on a screen while providing very different information to assistive technology. These examples demonstrate why authoring choices and mathematical structure matter.

About this demonstration: This page uses MathJax 4 to demonstrate modern mathematical accessibility features. Course platforms may use different MathJax versions, configurations, or equation editors. Follow the platform-specific guidance in the Michigan Virtual Style Guide when authoring course content.

1. Math Within a Sentence

Mathematical notation can remain within a sentence when that placement reads naturally. Creating math in an equation editor does not mean it needs to appear on its own line.

Plot the point (3,−2) on the coordinate plane. Then use d=r×t to calculate the distance traveled.

The coordinate and equation are structured math, but they still fit naturally within the surrounding sentence.

View MathML Structure 
<math>
  <mo>(</mo>
  <mn>3</mn>
  <mo>,</mo>
  <mo>−</mo>
  <mn>2</mn>
  <mo>)</mo>
</math>

<math>
  <mi>d</mi>
  <mo>=</mo>
  <mi>r</mi>
  <mi>t</mi>
</math>

2. Text Is Not a Variable

Simply typing letters in an equation editor identifies them as mathematical content. When letters form a word, label, abbreviation, or unit, use the editor's Text function.

Typed as ordinary math

60 mph

Here, m, p, and h are mathematical identifiers. Math accessibility tools may interpret relationships among the letters rather than the intended unit.

Using Text for the unit

60 mph

Here, mph is text. It represents the unit abbreviation for miles per hour rather than three mathematical variables.

View MathML Structure 
<!-- CourseArc/WIRIS: typed as ordinary math -->
<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML">
  <mn>60</mn>
  <mo>&#160;</mo>
  <mi>m</mi>
  <mi>p</mi>
  <mi>h</mi>
</math>

<!-- CourseArc/WIRIS: mph entered using Text -->
<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML">
  <mn>60</mn>
  <mo>&#160;</mo>
  <mtext>mph</mtext>
</math>

3. Structure Determines Meaning

Equation-editor templates create relationships between parts of an expression. These examples come directly from CourseArc's WIRIS equation editor and show how small authoring differences can change what the exponent is attached to.

The whole expression is squared

(x+2)2

Here, the entire parenthetical expression is grouped as the base of the superscript.

A different authoring attempt, same saved structure

(x+2)2

In this case, CourseArc/WIRIS normalized the author's input and saved the same MathML structure as the correctly authored version. This is useful to notice: different editing paths do not always result in different underlying math.

The exponent is attached to 2)

(x+2)2

This version can look deceptively close, but the superscript is structurally attached to the grouped content 2), not to the entire expression (x + 2).

The takeaway is not to memorize the MathML. When using superscripts, subscripts, fractions, radicals, or similar templates, make sure the intended content is in the correct part of the equation editor before moving on.

View MathML Structure 
<!-- CourseArc/WIRIS: whole expression squared -->
<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML">
  <msup>
    <mrow>
      <mo>(</mo>
      <mi>x</mi>
      <mo>+</mo>
      <mn>2</mn>
      <mo>)</mo>
    </mrow>
    <mn>2</mn>
  </msup>
</math>

<!-- WIRIS saved the same MathML as above. -->

<!-- CourseArc/WIRIS: exponent attached to 2) -->
<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML">

  <mi>x</mi>

4. Explore the Quadratic Formula

When mathematical relationships are structured correctly, MathJax can use that structure for speech and expression exploration.

Quadratic Formula: x = − b ± b2 − 4ac 2a

Quadratic Formula is identified as text, while the formula preserves nested mathematical relationships including a fraction, numerator, denominator, radical, exponent, operators, and variables.

Try the MathJax accessibility tools

  1. Move keyboard focus to the formula above, or right-click it with a mouse.
  2. Open the MathJax menu.
  3. Explore the available speech, Braille, and expression-exploration options.
  4. Move through individual parts of the formula and notice how the nested relationships are identified.

<math display="block">
  <mtext>Quadratic Formula:</mtext>
  <mi>x</mi>
  <mo>=</mo>
  <mfrac>
    <mrow>
      <mo>−</mo>
      <mi>b</mi>
      <mo>±</mo>
      <msqrt>
        <mrow>
          <msup>
            <mi>b</mi>
            <mn>2</mn>
          </msup>
          <mo>−</mo>
          <mn>4</mn>
          <mi>a</mi>
          <mi>c</mi>
        </mrow>
      </msqrt>
    </mrow>
    <mrow>
      <mn>2</mn>
      <mi>a</mi>
    </mrow>
  </mfrac>
</math>
MathML 101

You do not need to write MathML to create structured equations. Equation editors such as WIRIS generate this structure based on how you use the editor. These are some of the elements used in the examples above.

<mi>
A mathematical identifier, such as a variable.
<mn>
A number.
<mo>
A mathematical operator or delimiter, such as +, −, =, or parentheses.
<mtext>
Text within math, such as a word, label, abbreviation, or unit.
<mrow>
Groups related parts of an expression. It does not add a visible symbol by itself; it helps preserve which pieces belong together.
<msup>
Identifies a base and its superscript.
<msub>
Identifies a base and its subscript.
<msubsup>
Identifies a base with both a subscript and a superscript.
<mfrac>
Identifies the numerator and denominator of a fraction.
<msqrt>
Identifies the expression contained within a square root.
<mroot>
Identifies a radical with an explicit index, such as a cube root.

The important part: authors normally create these relationships by using the equation editor's fields, templates, symbols, and Text function correctly. Writing the MathML tags yourself is not required.

Remember: This page demonstrates what a modern MathJax implementation can do with structured mathematical content. It is not a substitute for platform-specific authoring guidance or accessibility testing.
